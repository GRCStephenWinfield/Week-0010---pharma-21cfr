# ADR-0058 — An objective is scored from the artefact its measure names, and from nothing else

## Context

**This is the decision that determines what the last phase of the programme is permitted to say about the
programme.**

The charter fixed a measure against every objective on 2026-03-06 and assigned a named individual owner to
each at `DEC-106` on 2026-03-20. Each measure names an artefact and describes the state that artefact would
be in if the objective were met. The charter went further and said so as a negative: *"No objective in this
set is measured by an opinion. There is no objective whose measure is a regulator's view, an auditor's view,
a consultant's assessment of maturity, or a score on a scale somebody sells."* — and it closed by requiring
that each objective be *"…scored from an artefact that either exists or does not."*

**The difficulty is that the artefact is not the only evidence available, and at close-out it is not the
most persuasive.** After more than fourteen months the organisation holds a very large amount of material
about how hard the work was, how far it got, what was attempted and what was intended: working papers,
minutes, monthly reports, enquiry returns, and the recollections of everybody who did any of it. **All of
that is real, and none of it is the artefact the measure names.**

**The pressure comes from the people who did the work, and it is not illegitimate.** An objective whose
artefact does not exist may nonetheless have absorbed most of a year, produced a method, changed how a
function thinks about its records, and stopped short of the artefact for a reason nobody in the programme
controlled. **Scoring it against the artefact alone records none of that**, and the individuals who own the
objectives are the individuals who will be scored.

**And the decider is the party with the strongest interest in a generous method.** The Programme Manager
owns the delivery of the programme, owns one of the objectives, and takes this decision **before any
objective was scored** — six days before, and the sequence is on the record at `GOV-41` because it is the
only thing that makes the method checkable rather than believable. **A method settled after the results are
known is a method chosen for the results it produces.**

## Decision

**An objective is scored from the artefact its measure names, and from nothing else. The score is binary:
met, or not met, with nothing between them.**

Four limbs.

**The artefact and only the artefact.** The scorer reads the artefact the measure names, in the state the
measure describes, and records whether it is in that state at the vantage. **Effort, progress, intent,
partial delivery, difficulty, external obstruction and the quality of the attempt are not inputs to the
score.** Where any of them explains a score, it belongs in the register's own column for what would change
the score, and it belongs there as a statement about the artefact.

**No opinion, from anybody.** No regulator's view, no auditor's view, no advisory firm's assessment of
maturity, no rating on a published scale, and no view held by the objective's owner about the objective's
owner's own work. **The charter's negative claim is not decoration and it is honoured literally.**

**The measure as the charter words it, and not as anybody would word it now.** The measure is transcribed
verbatim and checked against the published charter, and the score is taken against the transcription. **A
measure restated in the scorer's own words is a measure the scorer has quietly adjusted.**

**And the score is binary.** There is no partial value, no proportion, no band and no qualifier. Met means
the artefact is in the state the measure describes. Not met means it is not. **Nothing is subtracted from
*not met* for the work that went into it, and nothing is added to *met* for the ease of it.**

## Alternatives considered

**Score each objective on a three-value scale — met, partly met, not met — with the partial value defined
and evidenced, so that an objective which delivered most of what its measure asks is not recorded in the
same cell as one that delivered none of it.**

**This is the strongest alternative in the phase and it has to be put at its full strength, because it is
the option almost every programme of this kind takes and it is not taken out of laziness.**

**The first argument for it is that a binary score destroys information the organisation needs.** Two
objectives can both be *not met* and be in entirely different conditions: one may want a signature on a
document that is drafted, reviewed and sitting with an approver, and the other may want a state of the
estate that nobody has begun to bring about and nobody is funded to bring about. **Recording both as *not
met* tells whoever inherits them that they are the same size, which is false, and the register's own
what-would-change-the-score column is a thinner instrument than a scale.** The people who will pick this
work up are entitled to know which of it is nearly done.

**The second argument is that a partial score is not a soft score.** It can be defined as strictly as a
binary one. *Partly met* could be admitted only where the artefact the measure names exists, is under
document control, and is in the described state for an enumerated and countable subset of the population the
measure covers — a test that is read off the artefact exactly as the binary test is, with no opinion
anywhere in it. **On that definition the objection that partial scores invite judgement does not obviously
survive**, because the judgement has been replaced by an enumeration.

**The third argument is fairness to the owners, and it is the one that has moral weight.** Each objective
has a named individual owner. **A binary score attaches the same word to an owner who delivered a method,
an assessment, a register and a specification, and stopped one approval short of the artefact, as it does to
an owner who did nothing** — and the repository has spent nine phases insisting that its unit of
accountability is a person. Having insisted on that, it then scores the person's objective on an instrument
that cannot tell the two cases apart.

**And the fourth is that the charter does not forbid it.** The charter's negative claim forbids scoring by
opinion. **A partial value defined by enumeration is not an opinion**, and reading the charter as though it
had also fixed the number of permitted values is reading a prohibition into a sentence that states a
different one.

**Refused, on three grounds.**

**A partial score is the only score anybody argues with, and the argument is unwinnable.** A binary score
turns on whether an artefact is in a described state, which is a question with an answer somebody can be
shown. **The moment a middle value exists, every score that would have been *not met* becomes a negotiation
about whether it is really *partly met*** — and that negotiation is conducted at close-out, by the people
who own the objectives, with the whole organisation wanting the phase to read better than it does. **The
enumerated definition does not survive it**, because the argument moves one step back and becomes an
argument about what the population is, which is exactly the kind of argument a measure fixed on 2026-03-06
exists to prevent.

**The information the binary score loses is recoverable and is recovered elsewhere; the property it buys is
not recoverable at all.** Every score in this phase is published beside the artefact it was taken from and
beside a statement of what would change it, and `../09.02-the-charter-scored.md` argues each one at length.
**A reader who wants to know how close an objective came can read that; a reader who wants to know whether
it was met reads one word, and no amount of reading changes the word.** **The binary score loses
information and buys something worth more: nobody can argue a score up.**

**And the charter's measures were not written for a scale.** Each measure describes a state, not a
proportion — an artefact that exists under document control, a register with no entries of a described
kind, a demonstration witnessed by a named person, a variance stated as a figure. **A measure written as a
state has exactly two values by construction**, and supplying a third at close-out would be supplying it
after the results are known, to a measure whose author cannot be asked. `01.06 §9` fixed that nobody may
vary a measure during the programme, and **inventing a value the measure does not have is varying it by
another route.**

---

**Score the objectives against what the programme could reasonably have achieved, rather than against what
the charter asked for — that is, hold the measure constant but state the score in the light of the
constraints the programme met.**

**A serious argument, and it is the one the owners make.** Several objectives require a change to systems
the programme neither owned nor was funded to change, and an objective that could not have been met by any
act available to its owner is an objective that was mis-set rather than missed. **Scoring it as though the
owner had simply failed to do it records something untrue about why it stands where it stands**, and the
repository's own discipline elsewhere is to record the reason rather than the appearance.

**Refused.** **A score against what was achievable is a score against a counterfactual, and a
counterfactual is an opinion with a plan attached.** Somebody has to decide what was reasonably achievable,
and that somebody is inside the programme. **The charter's negative claim is aimed at exactly this move.**
The reason a score stands where it stands is recorded — every row of the register carries what would change
the score, and `../09.03-what-the-score-is-shaped-like.md` takes the pattern in the scores as its subject.
**The reason is published beside the score and does not enter it.**

---

**Publish no score at all — report against the objectives in narrative, describing the position of each
without reducing it to a word.**

**The most honest-looking option**, and its point is real: a word is a compression, compressions travel, and
the compression is what will be quoted. A narrative cannot be misquoted as a verdict because it is not one.

**Refused, on two grounds.** **The charter promised a score and the promise was the discipline.** The
measures were written to be readable from evidence precisely so that the programme could not report on
itself in prose at the end, and **a programme that fixes scoreable measures and then declines to score them
has taken the benefit of the discipline and avoided its cost.** **And a narrative is not the safer
instrument it appears to be.** A narrative about an objective that was not met is written by people who
would like it to be read kindly; a word is not. **The score is the part that cannot be softened in the
drafting, which is the whole reason for having one.**

## Consequences

**Accepted — the register records objectives as not met that absorbed a great deal of work**, and the
register does not say so in the score. It says so beside the score, in a column, once.

**Accepted — the score is unflattering to the programme that designed the scoring method**, and the method
was fixed before any objective was looked at so that the reader does not have to take that on trust.

**Accepted — a binary score will be read as harsher than the underlying position**, and no qualifier is
available anywhere to soften it. **The owners of the objectives were not consulted on the method after the
scores were known, and were not consulted on the scores after the method was fixed.**

**Accepted — the score is a score and not a verdict**, and a reader who takes it as one has taken it
further than it goes. `../09.12-what-this-does-not-establish.md` states that in terms.

**Gained — no score in this phase can be argued up**, because there is nothing to argue about except
whether an artefact is in a described state, and the artefact is named.

**Gained — the measure, the artefact and the score sit in one row of one register**, so that a reader who
disagrees with a score knows exactly what to go and look at.

## Refused

**This decision forbids the following in Phase 09 and binds any later reader of its registers.**

- **Scoring any objective from effort, progress, intent, difficulty, partial delivery, external obstruction
  or the quality of the attempt.**
- **Scoring any objective from an opinion of any kind** — a regulator's, an auditor's, an advisory firm's,
  a maturity assessment, a rating on a published scale, or the objective owner's own view of the objective
  owner's own work.
- **Admitting any score other than met or not met** — no partial value, no proportion, no band, no
  percentage, no qualifier, no adverb and no hedge, **under any label and in any register, chapter, diagram,
  governance record or workbook of this phase.**
- **Restating, paraphrasing, narrowing, widening or modernising any measure**, or scoring against anything
  other than the measure as the charter published it.
- **Producing an aggregate, an index, a total score, a proportion or a trend from the scores.**
- **Varying a measure at close-out**, by amendment or by supplying a value the measure does not have —
  `01.06 §9`.
- **Treating a score as a verdict, a finding, a deviation, an inspectional observation, a non-compliance, a
  compliance conclusion or a legal conclusion of any kind.**
- **Reading a score as a statement about the individual who owns the objective.** **No document in this
  repository assesses any individual's work.**
