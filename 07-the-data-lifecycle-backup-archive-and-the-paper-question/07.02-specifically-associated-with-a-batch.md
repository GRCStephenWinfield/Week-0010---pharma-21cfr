## 1. What this chapter settles

**This chapter reads `21 CFR 211.180` and states what it says.** It reads all five of its paragraphs,
because the section is a whole and the industry habit of quoting one paragraph of it and moving on is the
reason so few firms can say where their retention periods come from.

**It states no result.** How Helix's in-scope record types fall against this section is
`07.03-the-derivation.md`'s and `logs/retention-derivation-register.md`'s; what follows from a record
type having no period at all is `07.04-what-nothing-sets.md`'s; the location question at
`21 CFR 211.180(c)` is `07.06-where-the-records-are-and-where-the-activity-was.md`'s; and
`21 CFR 211.180(d)` read beside FDA's ALCOA vocabulary is `07.11-original-or-a-true-copy-on-the-cgmp-side.md`'s.
**No count of any kind appears in this chapter, and none may be added to it.** The method — that a period
is derived and never chosen — is `07.01-a-period-is-derived-not-chosen.md`'s and is not re-argued here.

What this chapter does argue, at length, is one clause: **the words *"specifically associated with a
batch of a drug product"* in `21 CFR 211.180(a)`, what they reach, what they do not reach, and why a firm that applies the
paragraph's period to a record it does not reach has invented an obligation and then claimed credit for
meeting it.** `ADR-0045` records the decision that follows and `§12` sets it out.

And it settles one further thing, because the misunderstanding is universal and it is at the centre of this
phase: **`21 CFR Part 11` sets no retention period of its own.** `§11` quotes the two paragraphs of Part 11
that mention retention, whole, and shows that one of them is a relation whose second term comes from
somewhere else and the other is an obligation whose endpoint comes from the same place.

## 2. The section, read whole

**`21 CFR 211.180` is titled *General requirements* and sits in Subpart J — Records and Reports.** It has
paragraphs `(a)` through `(e)`, and they do five different jobs. Setting them out side by side before
reading any of them closely is worth the space, because the commonest error in this area is to treat the
section as though it were one rule about how long to keep things.

| ¶ | What it does | The kind of provision it is |
|---|---|---|
| **`(a)`** | Sets a retention period for production, control and distribution records **that are specifically associated with a batch** | **A period, with a condition on what it reaches** |
| **`(b)`** | Sets a retention period for records of components, drug product containers, closures and labelling | **A period, for a different class of record** |
| **`(c)`** | Requires records required under Part 211, or copies of them, to be readily available for authorised inspection **during the retention period**, at the establishment where the activities occurred; requires those records or copies to be **subject to photocopying or other means of reproduction as part of such inspection**; and saves the position where the records can be immediately retrieved electronically from another location | **An availability obligation and a reproduction obligation, whose duration is set by something else** |
| **`(d)`** | Permits records to be retained **either as original records or as true copies** | **A permission, conditioned on the copy being true** |
| **`(e)`** | Requires records to be maintained so that the data in them can be used to evaluate, at least annually, the quality standards of each drug product **to determine the need for changes in drug product specifications or manufacturing or control procedures** | **A content-and-availability obligation that sets no period of its own for the record it requires** |

**Three of the five say nothing at all about how long anything is kept.** `(c)` presupposes a retention
period and does not supply one. `(d)` is about form, not duration. `(e)` is about use, and about the review
it requires, and is silent on how long the review record itself survives.

**Only `(a)` and `(b)` set periods, and neither of them sets a period for everything.** That is the whole
of the chapter in one sentence, and everything below is the working.

**Read whole means quoted, and each paragraph is quoted where it is read:** `(a)`'s opening words at `§3`,
`(b)` in full at `§6`, `(c)`'s three sentences at `§7`, `(d)`'s first sentence at `§8`, and `(e)`'s first
sentence at `§9`. **Two passages are stated in this repository's own words rather than quoted — the two
period limbs that follow `(a)`'s opening words, and `(d)`'s reduction-techniques sentence — and the
chapter says so at each of them rather than letting a paraphrase pass for a quotation.**

## 3. `21 CFR 211.180(a)` — the paragraph, and the clause that does the work

**The opening words, verbatim:**

> *"Any production, control, or distribution record that is required to be maintained in compliance with
> this part **and is specifically associated with a batch of a drug product**…"*

The paragraph goes on to fix the period. **A record within its reach is retained for at least one year
after the expiration date of the batch; and where the drug product is one of the OTC drug products that
lack expiration dating because they meet the criteria for exemption under `21 CFR 211.137`, for three years
after distribution of the batch.** Those are the paragraph's two limbs, and this repository states them in its own words rather
than quoting them, because the quotation Helix holds of this paragraph runs to the words emphasised above
and no further.

**Read the opening for its grammar.** It contains a subject — *any production, control, or distribution
record* — and then **two conditions joined by "and"**, both of which must hold before the period attaches:

| # | Condition | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | The record is **required to be maintained in compliance with this part** | Does some provision of `21 CFR Part 211` require this record to exist and be kept? |
| **2** | The record **is specifically associated with a batch of a drug product** | Is this record about one identified batch of a drug product, rather than about a process, a period, a room, a person or a method? |

**And the three words at the end of that clause are load-bearing.** The batch the paragraph speaks of is a
batch **of a drug product**, not a lot of a component, a consignment of containers or a delivery of
labelling. **That is why `(b)` exists at all** — a record about a component is not brought inside `(a)` by
the component having been consumed in a batch, and a derivation that reaches for `(a)` on a component
record has read the clause to the word *batch* and stopped three words early.

**The conditions are conjunctive, and the second one is the one nobody reads.** A record can satisfy the
first perfectly — it can be squarely required by Part 211, it can be a production record in the plainest
sense, it can be the sort of thing an investigator would ask for on the first morning — **and still not be
reached by this paragraph's period, because it is not specifically associated with a batch.**

**And notice the adverb.** The paragraph does not say *associated with a batch*; it says **specifically**
associated with a batch. Almost every record a drug manufacturer keeps is associated with batches in some
loose sense: a cleaning record is associated with every batch made on the equipment after it, a training
record is associated with every batch the trained person touched, a specification is associated with every
batch tested against it. **If loose association were enough, the qualifier would do no work, and a reading
that makes a word of a regulation do no work is a reading to be suspicious of.**

**The clause reaches the records whose subject is a particular batch.** The batch production and control
record of batch X; the in-process control result taken on batch X; the release testing record for batch X;
the distribution record showing where batch X went. **Each of those has a batch as its subject, and each of
them acquires, from that batch, the very thing the paragraph needs in order to run a clock at all — an
expiration date.**

**That is the mechanism, and it is worth stating explicitly because it explains the qualifier rather than
merely reciting it.** `21 CFR 211.180(a)`'s period is *at least one year after the expiration date of the
batch*. **A period expressed that way can only be computed for a record that has a batch, because only a
batch has an expiration date.** The qualifier is not a policy choice FDA made about which records deserve
protection. **It is the condition under which the paragraph's own arithmetic is capable of producing an
answer.**

**A record that spans batches has no expiration date to count from, and `21 CFR 211.180(a)` therefore
supplies it with no period at all.** Not a short one. Not a default one. None.

## 4. What the clause excludes, and what that means

Four classes of record illustrate the exclusion. **None of them is named against any Helix record type
here, and this chapter states nothing about which Helix record types fall where** —
`07.03-the-derivation.md` and `logs/retention-derivation-register.md` carry that and nothing else does.
These are read as classes of record, in the abstract, against the paragraph's words.

**A record of an equipment's cleaning and use across a sequence of batches.** Take it as given that the
record is one Part 211 requires to be maintained, so that condition one is satisfied and only the second is
in issue. Its subject is a piece of equipment across a sequence of uses: it records that the equipment was
cleaned, by whom, when, and what was made on it before and after. **It is associated with every batch that
touched the equipment and specifically associated with none of them.** Which batch's expiration date would
the clock run from? The paragraph offers no way to choose, and a provision that cannot identify its own
starting point has not set a period.

**The distinction that carries this class is between an entry and a record.** A single line in such a log
— *this equipment, this batch, this date and time* — is about one batch and nothing else. **The log is
not.** `21 CFR 211.180(a)` speaks of a *record*, and the record is the log; the entry is a part of it, and
a period cannot attach to a part of a record while the record it sits in has none. **A reader who
collapses the two will find batch association everywhere and the qualifier will do no work at all.**

**A trend computed over a date range.** A trend is a query over a data set spanning a date range — a set of
environmental monitoring results, a set of storage-condition readings, a set of in-process values. Its
subject is the range. Individual points inside it may each belong to a batch; **the trend does not, and the
trend is the record.** The same reasoning applies with more force here than to the cleaning record, because
a trend whose range is redrawn is a different record with different contents.

**A record whose subject is a person.** A record of an individual's education, training, experience or
qualification has a person as its subject. **A person is not a batch**, and no reading of the paragraph
attaches a batch's expiration date to a record about somebody's qualifications. **Whether Part 211 requires
any such record to be maintained, and under which provision, is an applicability question and is Phase
02's** — this chapter reads only what `(a)` reaches.

**A criterion batches are tested against.** A specification, a standard or a test method is the thing
batches are measured against. **It is the yardstick, not the measurement**, and the yardstick outlives every
measurement made with it. It is associated with every batch tested against it, which is precisely why it is
specifically associated with none.

**Set out as a table, because the shape of the exclusion matters more than any one example:**

| The record's subject | Does `21 CFR 211.180(a)` reach it? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **One identified batch** | **Yes**, where the record is also required under Part 211 | Both conditions hold, and the batch supplies the expiration date the period counts from |
| **A piece of equipment, across uses** | **No** | Associated with many batches; specifically associated with none; no single expiration date |
| **A date range or a population** | **No** | The subject is the range, and the range has no expiration date |
| **A person** | **No** | The subject is a person's qualification, and it survives every batch that person worked on |
| **A method, a specification or a standard** | **No** | The subject is the criterion, not the thing measured |
| **The firm's own procedures and their governance** | **No** | The subject is how the firm operates, not what it made on a given day |

**And here is the sentence that has to be held steadily, because it is uncomfortable and it is correct:
being outside `21 CFR 211.180(a)`'s reach is not a statement that the record matters less.** Several of
the classes above are more consequential than a single batch record, because they govern every batch rather
than one. **The paragraph is not ranking importance. It is stating the class of record for which it is able
to express a period, and it is silent about the rest.**

## 5. What `(a)` does with the records it does reach

Two limbs, and Helix reads both because a firm that reads only the first will get a class of product wrong.

**The ordinary limb.** A record within `(a)`'s reach is retained for **at least one year after the
expiration date of the batch**. Three properties of that formulation matter.

**It is a floor, not a ceiling.** *At least* one year after the expiration date of the batch. **The
regulation sets a minimum and says nothing about a maximum**, and a firm that retains for longer has not
departed from it. That asymmetry is the
one place in this whole area where a firm has genuine latitude, and it is latitude to keep records longer,
never shorter.

**It counts from expiry, not from creation.** A record made at the start of manufacture, for a product with
a long shelf life, is retained for the whole of that shelf life plus a year. **The period is therefore a
property of the product as much as of the record**, and two records of identical type on two products can
carry very different retention dates. A retention schedule that expresses this period as a number of years
from creation has silently substituted a different rule for the regulation's.

**And the clock has a stated event, which means it can be audited.** *The expiration date of the batch* is
a date somebody can look up. **A period whose starting event is identifiable is a period that can be tested
against a record; a period expressed as a bare number of years cannot be, because nothing says when to
start counting.**

**The exempt OTC limb.** Where the drug product is one of the OTC drug products that lack expiration dating
because they meet the criteria for exemption, the period runs **three years after distribution of the
batch** instead — **the exemption being the one at `21 CFR 211.137`, which `(a)` and `(b)` both cross-refer
to by section number.** **The limb exists for a reason that follows directly from the mechanism at `§3`: where
there is no expiration date, a period expressed as one year after expiry cannot be computed, so the
regulation supplies a different starting event.** It is the same drafting logic that produced the
*specifically associated with a batch* condition in the first place, applied to a different failure of the
clock.

**Helix manufactures oral solid dose pharmaceuticals**, and whether any of its products falls within that
exemption is a product-regulatory question and not one this chapter answers. **The limb is stated because a
chapter that reads a paragraph whole reads all of it**, and because a derivation that ignored it would be
wrong for any product to which it applied.

## 6. `21 CFR 211.180(b)` — the second period, for a different class

**`(b)`, verbatim, because it is one of the two paragraphs of this section that is usually paraphrased and
never read:**

> *"Records shall be maintained for all components, drug product containers, closures, and labeling for at
> least 1 year after the expiration date or, in the case of certain OTC drug products lacking expiration
> dating because they meet the criteria for exemption under § 211.137, 3 years after distribution of the
> last lot of drug product incorporating the component or using the container, closure, or labeling."*

**`(b)` covers records for components, drug product containers, closures and labelling.** Records of that
class are retained for **at least one year after the expiration date**, and in the exempt OTC case, for
**three years after distribution of the last lot of drug product incorporating the component or using the
container, closure or labelling**.

Four things to note, and the third is the one that gets missed.

**The exempt OTC limb has a stated trigger and it is not the bare phrase *three years after
distribution*.** It is three years after distribution of **the last lot** of drug product incorporating the
component or using the container, closure or labelling. **A duration without its event is not a period**,
and this one's event is later than a careless reading makes it: an excipient consumed across several
products runs to the last of them.

**It is a second period, not an extension of the first.** `(b)` is its own provision with its own class of
record and its own trigger. A derivation that cites `(a)` for a component record has cited the wrong
paragraph and will not survive being asked which words it relies on.

**Its trigger is the expiry of the drug product that incorporated the component**, not the expiry of the
component itself and not the date the component was received. **A lot of excipient may be consumed across
several drug product batches, and the record's period is governed by the products those batches became.**

**And `(b)` is proof that the section's structure is deliberate.** FDA did not write a paragraph reaching
batch-associated records and then leave everything else to inference; it wrote a *second* paragraph for a
second class it wanted reached. **A regulator that specifies two classes has told you something about the
classes it did not specify**, and the something is not that they are covered by analogy.

**`(a)` and `(b)` are also not the only provisions in `21 CFR Part 211` that set a retention period.**
`21 CFR 211.198(b)`, on product complaint records, sets one that is not in `21 CFR 211.180` at all. **A
derivation therefore reads the record's own predicate rule before it reads `21 CFR 211.180`**, because the
period may be sitting in the provision that required the record in the first place.
`07.01-a-period-is-derived-not-chosen.md §5` owns that method point; this chapter records only that the
section is not the exclusive home of a CGMP retention period.

## 7. `21 CFR 211.180(c)` — availability, and an endpoint borrowed from elsewhere

**The first sentence, verbatim:**

> *"All records required under this part, or copies of such records, shall be readily available for
> authorized inspection during the retention period at the establishment where the activities described in
> such records occurred."*

Four obligations are packed into that sentence and they are usually read as one.

**Readily available.** Not merely retained. Not merely locatable in principle. **Available for inspection,
readily**, which is a property of an arrangement and not of a storage medium.

**Or copies of such records.** The sentence anticipates that what is produced may be a copy, which is the
hook `(d)` hangs on. **The two paragraphs are designed to be read together**, and
`07.11-original-or-a-true-copy-on-the-cgmp-side.md` is where that reading is done.

**During the retention period.** **This is the borrowed endpoint.** `(c)` does not set a retention period;
it uses one. Where a record's period comes from `(a)` or `(b)`, `(c)`'s obligation has a definite duration.
**Where no provision sets a period for the record, `(c)` still applies and its duration is undefined** —
which is not a licence to stop, and is not a period of zero, and is not anything at all except an
unanswered question about the firm's own records.

**At the establishment where the activities described in such records occurred.** A location obligation,
expressed in terms of the activity rather than the record. **The place that matters is where the work was
done, not where the server is.** How that lands on Helix's estate is
`07.06-where-the-records-are-and-where-the-activity-was.md`'s and nothing in this chapter reaches it.

**The paragraph's second sentence, verbatim, and it is the one that is routinely dropped:**

> *"These records or copies thereof shall be subject to photocopying or other means of reproduction as part
> of such inspection."*

**This is a second obligation and not a restatement of the first.** Availability is a state; reproduction
is an act, performed during the inspection, on the record or on a copy of it. **A firm whose records are
readily available but which cannot reproduce them during an inspection has met the first sentence and not
the second** — and the sentence is the textual bridge to `(d)`, because a paragraph that contemplates the
copy being made during inspection is a paragraph written alongside one permitting the retained thing to be
a copy.

**And the paragraph's third and final sentence is the saving, verbatim:**

> *"Records that can be immediately retrieved from another location by computer or other electronic means
> shall be considered as meeting the requirements of this paragraph."*

**Read the saving for its condition.** It is not *records held electronically* and it is not *records
accessible from another location*. It is **records that can be immediately retrieved**. **The saving is
conditional on a demonstrated retrieval capability**, and a firm relying on it is asserting a fact about
its own systems — a fact that can be demonstrated, timed and recorded, and which, until it is, is an
assertion rather than a saving.

**Note also what the saving does and does not do.** It saves the *location* requirement. **It does not
extend, shorten or supply the retention period**, and it does not touch the readily-available requirement
except by supplying an acceptable means of meeting it. **And it does not reach the second sentence at
all:** a firm relying on immediate retrieval from elsewhere is still a firm whose records or copies of them
must be capable of being photocopied or otherwise reproduced as part of the inspection. A firm that reads
the sentence as *electronic records are fine* has read four words of it.

## 8. `21 CFR 211.180(d)` — the permission

**The first sentence, verbatim:**

> *"Records required under this part may be retained either as original records or as true copies such as
> photocopies, microfilm, microfiche, or other accurate reproductions of the original records."*

**And the paragraph does not end there.** Its next sentence addresses reduction techniques: where such
techniques, microfilming among them, are used, suitable reader and photocopying equipment must be readily
available. **This repository states that sentence in its own words rather than quoting it**, and states it
at all because a paragraph read to its first full stop is a paragraph read to the point where it stopped
being convenient.

**The reduction-techniques sentence is not an antiquarian detail.** It is the drafting evidence that the
paragraph is about a *retained form that somebody must still be able to read and reproduce*, and the
principle transfers to every medium that has arrived since: **a permission to retain in a reduced or
converted form carries with it an obligation to keep whatever is needed to render it.** A firm holding
records in a proprietary format whose reader it no longer licenses is in the same position as a firm
holding microfiche and no microfiche reader.

**That transfer is not this repository's inference, and it is worth saying whose it is.** FDA made it in
the December 2018 drug CGMP data integrity questions and answers, reading *suitable reader and copying
equipment* as reaching *"software and hardware, including media readers"* and citing `21 CFR 211.180(d)`
while doing so. **That is guidance and it is cited as guidance; the requirement is the paragraph's.** The
quotation, its context and what follows from it belong to
`07.11-original-or-a-true-copy-on-the-cgmp-side.md` and are set out there.

**What `(d)` is, structurally, is a permission conditioned on the copy being true**, and **the argument
about what that condition requires, what FDA has and has not said about how a copy is shown to be true, and
how the paragraph reads beside FDA's ALCOA vocabulary, belongs to
`07.11-original-or-a-true-copy-on-the-cgmp-side.md` and is made there and nowhere else.** This chapter
records the text and its place in the section, and stops.

## 9. `21 CFR 211.180(e)` — the annual evaluation, and the period it does not set

**`(e)`'s first sentence, verbatim, because the clause at the end of it is the one that is habitually
dropped:**

> *"Written records required by this part shall be maintained so that data therein can be used for
> evaluating, at least annually, the quality standards of each drug product to determine the need for
> changes in drug product specifications or manufacturing or control procedures."*

**`(e)` requires records to be maintained so that data in them can be used to evaluate, at least annually,
the quality standards of each drug product — and it says what the evaluation is for: to determine the need
for changes in drug product specifications or in manufacturing or control procedures.** The evaluation
draws on a representative number of batches, whether approved or rejected, and where applicable the records
associated with the batch — `(e)(1)` — and on a review of complaints, recalls, returned or salvaged drug
products, **and investigations conducted under `21 CFR 211.192`** — `(e)(2)`, which cross-refers to that
section by number. **It requires the evaluation to be done and it requires written procedures for it.**

**And it sets no retention period for the record the evaluation produces.**

**That is worth sitting with, because it is the section's own demonstration of the point this chapter
exists to make.** The paragraph that requires an annual product quality evaluation — one of the most
consequential quality records a drug manufacturer produces, the document in which the firm states its own
conclusion about whether its product is performing to standard — **is in the same section as two paragraphs
that set periods, and it sets none.**

**The evaluation record is also, on its face, not a record specifically associated with a batch.** It is
associated with a product across a year and with a representative number of batches. **So `(a)` does not
reach it either**, and the point is structural rather than incidental: **the section that sets the drug
CGMP retention periods contains, within itself, a required record for which it sets no period.**

`(e)` also does something else that bears on this phase and is easy to miss. **It expresses a
requirement about how records are *maintained*, in terms of what they must be capable of supporting.**
Records maintained so that the data in them **can be used** for the evaluation. A record that survives in a
form from which the data cannot be extracted, aggregated or read has not been maintained the way `(e)`
requires, however faithfully it has been stored. **Retention and usability are two obligations and this
paragraph is where they meet on the CGMP side.**

## 10. The reading this programme does not adopt, argued fairly

**The alternative is real, it is what a great many careful firms do, and it is not stupid.** It has to be
put at its strongest before it is refused, because a refusal that argues against a weak version of the
alternative has not refused anything.

**The alternative: apply the longest applicable period to everything.**

Take the longest period any provision of Part 211 sets for any record the firm holds — in practice, a
period derived from `21 CFR 211.180(a)` on the longest-dated product — and apply it to every regulated
record, including those no provision reaches. **Write it into a retention schedule, apply it uniformly, and
sleep better.**

**Four arguments for it, each of which is genuinely good.**

**It cannot under-retain.** Whatever the correct period turns out to be for any record, the longest
applicable period is at least as long. **A firm that applies it will never be found to have destroyed a
record it was required to keep**, which is the failure mode with consequences.

**It is administratively simple, and simplicity is a control.** One rule, applied by everybody, is a rule
that gets applied. A schedule with dozens of distinct derivations is a schedule that will be misapplied at
the edges, and every misapplication at the edges is a potential under-retention. **A crude rule that is
followed can outperform a precise rule that is not.**

**It removes a class of judgement from people who should not be exercising it.** Under a derived scheme,
somebody at the point of disposal has to know which class a record falls in. Under the uniform rule,
nobody does. **Removing discretion from a destruction decision is a defensible piece of quality
engineering.**

**And storage is cheap.** The economic argument that historically justified aggressive disposal has largely
evaporated for electronic records. **If keeping everything for the longest period costs little, why derive
anything at all?**

**The programme does not adopt it, for three reasons, and the first is decisive on its own.**

**A period applied by analogy has no basis to be tested against.** This is the whole of it. Ask the
question an inspector or an internal auditor asks: *why this period?* Under a derived scheme the answer is a
citation — this provision, these words, this trigger event, this record type — **and the answer can be
checked, argued with, and found wrong.** Under the uniform rule the answer is *because it is the longest
one we found somewhere else*. **That is not a basis. It is a habit with a number attached**, and it cannot
be audited, because there is nothing to audit it against.

**And the consequence is not that the firm is over-cautious. It is that the firm has lost the ability to
notice when it is wrong.** If the uniform period is later shortened — for cost, for storage, for a system
migration, for a decommissioning — **there is no derivation to consult about whether the shorter period is
lawful for any particular record.** The firm has no way of knowing which of its records the change breaks,
because it never established what any of them required. **The uniform rule is safe only while it is
generous, and nothing in it records why it must stay generous.**

**Second: it conceals the records for which nothing sets a period, and those are the ones that need a
decision.** Where no predicate rule supplies a period, somebody at Helix must decide one, on a stated
basis, with a name against it — `07.01-a-period-is-derived-not-chosen.md` owns that argument and
`ADR-0044` records it. **Applying a borrowed period to those records makes the gap invisible.** The record
acquires a number, the schedule looks complete, and the decision that was owed is never taken.
**A gap covered by an analogy is worse than a gap left open, because only the second one gets fixed.**

**Third: it misdescribes the regulation, and misdescription has a way of travelling.** A schedule that
prints *`21 CFR 211.180(a)`* in the basis column against a record the paragraph does not reach has made a
false statement about a federal regulation, in a controlled document, that somebody will later rely on.
**The firm is not merely doing more than it must; it is saying something untrue about what it must do**,
and the untrue statement is exactly the kind that survives into a procedure, a validation rationale and
eventually an inspection response.

**What the programme does instead, and it concedes the alternative's strongest point.** **Nothing in this
reading prevents Helix from retaining any record for longer than any derived period.** `(a)`'s period is a
floor. **A firm may keep records as long as it likes; what it may not do is record the reason as a
regulation that does not say so.** The derivation establishes what is required. **What Helix elects to do
beyond that is a decision, is recorded as a decision, and carries a name** — and that is the difference
between a position and a practice.

## 11. And `21 CFR Part 11` sets no retention period at all

**This is the sentence the whole phase turns on and it is stated here, in the chapter that reads the
regulations, so that no other chapter has to argue it.**

**`21 CFR Part 11` contains ten sections in three subparts. Not one of them sets a retention period.** Two
of them mention retention, and both of them borrow.

**`21 CFR 11.10(e)`, whole, verbatim:**

> *"Use of secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails to independently record the date and time
> of operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete electronic records. Record changes shall
> not obscure previously recorded information. Such audit trail documentation shall be retained for a
> period at least as long as that required for the subject electronic records and shall be available for
> agency review and copying."*

**The third sentence is a `shall`, and it is a relation with two terms.** *Retained for a period at least as
long as that required for the subject electronic records.* **The first term is the audit trail
documentation. The second term is the period required for the record the audit trail describes** — and
that period comes from the predicate rule, because Part 11 does not supply one.

**The audit trail therefore inherits the retention period of its record. It does not have one of its own,
and Part 11 does not give it one.** What follows for Helix's estate, where the second term exists and where
it does not, is `07.09-a-relation-with-two-terms.md`'s, and this chapter states no position on it.

**`21 CFR 11.10(c)`, whole, verbatim:**

> *"Protection of records to enable their accurate and ready retrieval throughout the records retention
> period."*

**Fourteen words, and the last four are the ones that matter here.** *Throughout the records retention
period.* **The obligation is bounded by an endpoint the paragraph does not define.** Part 11 requires the
protection to last as long as the retention period; **the retention period is the predicate rule's, and
where the predicate rule sets none, the paragraph's obligation has no stated endpoint.** What that means
for the determinations Phase 05 published is `07.08-the-endpoint-that-does-not-exist.md`'s, and **this
chapter re-determines nothing and states nothing about any cell.**

**Set the two beside each other and the structure is identical.**

| Paragraph | What it requires | Where its duration comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **`21 CFR 11.10(c)`** | Protection enabling accurate and ready retrieval | **The records retention period** — set by the predicate rule |
| **`21 CFR 11.10(e)`**, third sentence | Retention of the audit trail documentation | **A period at least as long as that required for the subject electronic records** — set by the predicate rule |

**Both are downstream of the predicate rule, and neither can be satisfied, or breached, or even evaluated,
until the predicate rule's period has been established.** That is why this phase reads `21 CFR 211.180`
before it reads anything of Part 11, and it is why `21 CFR 11.10(e)`'s retention limb had been an open
question in this programme since Phase 01 without anybody being able to make progress on it: **nobody had
established the second term.**

**And the negative claim, stated in the form the repository uses everywhere:** **no document in this
repository says that `21 CFR Part 11` sets a retention period, requires a retention schedule, or specifies
how long any record is kept.** It does none of those things. **A firm that has met every requirement in
Part 11 has still not established how long a single record must be retained**, and a firm that believes
otherwise has attributed to Part 11 an obligation that lives in Part 211.

## 12. `ADR-0045` — a record not specifically associated with a batch is not reached

**`ADR-0045`, taken at `DEC-705` on 2027-03-05 by Dr Marguerite Oyelaran, records that a record not
specifically associated with a batch is not reached by `21 CFR 211.180(a)`'s period, and that the
programme records that fact rather than applying the period by analogy.**

Four limbs.

**The clause is applied as written, to every record type, on the record type's own subject matter.** The
question asked is *what is this record about?* — and where the answer is anything other than one identified
batch, `21 CFR 211.180(a)` does not supply its period. **The question is not whether the record is
important, whether it is laboratory or manufacturing, or whether an inspector would ask for it.**

**Where `(a)` does not reach a record, the derivation looks for another provision and does not borrow.**
`21 CFR 211.180(b)`, or a period set in the record's own predicate rule, or — where neither exists — the
recorded statement that no predicate rule sets a period at all.

**Where nothing sets a period, that is recorded as the position and no period is supplied.** Not by
analogy, not as a default, not as a range, not as a bound and not as a recommendation.
**`07.04-what-nothing-sets.md` carries what follows; `ADR-0044` carries who owes the decision;
`ADR-0050` carries that this phase does not take it.**

**And no register column in this phase prints a citation the record does not rest on.** A basis column
carries the provision actually relied on or it carries the word *none*. **A citation entered for the sake
of completing a column is a false statement about a regulation**, and *a response column completed for the
sake of completion is worse than an empty one, because it stops anybody looking.*

**What the decision costs, accepted openly.** It produces a derivation in which a substantial part of the
estate's record types carry no period, which is a less comfortable document than a complete-looking
schedule. **It converts what could have been presented as a solved problem into a recorded gap with a named
individual attached.** And it means Helix cannot answer *how long do you keep everything?* with a single
number. **The programme accepts all three, because the alternative is a schedule whose basis column is
partly fiction.**

## 13. What this chapter does not establish

- **It states no count, no partition and no figure of any kind.** How Helix's in-scope record types fall
  against `21 CFR 211.180` is `07.03-the-derivation.md`'s and
  `logs/retention-derivation-register.md`'s. **Nothing in this chapter may be cited for a number.**
- **It names no record type**, and it states nothing about which group any record type falls in.
- **It attaches no predicate-rule anchor to any in-scope record type, because an anchor is an identifier
  and the identifiers are `07.03-the-derivation.md`'s and the register's.** Where a provision of Part 211
  other than `21 CFR 211.180` is named here it is named as a provision of the section's neighbourhood and
  against no record type at all.
- **It sets no retention period for anything**, proposes none, bounds none and suggests no range —
  `ADR-0050`.
- **It states no retention policy, no retention schedule, no archive design, no migration plan, no storage
  specification and no vendor.**
- **It reaches no conclusion about `21 CFR 211.180(c)`'s location requirement on any Helix system**, and
  demonstrates, times or records no retrieval — `07.06-where-the-records-are-and-where-the-activity-was.md`.
- **It makes no argument about `21 CFR 211.180(d)` beyond recording its text and its place in the
  section** — `07.11-original-or-a-true-copy-on-the-cgmp-side.md` owns that argument, and **the numeral
  coincidence that chapter names is not reached here at all.**
- **It re-opens no Phase 02 applicability determination.** **The printout route holds for the four record
  types Phase 02 published as out of scope, and this chapter disturbs it in no respect** —
  `07.10-the-paper-question.md`, `ADR-0046`.
- **It re-determines no Phase 05 control position, re-scores no Phase 03 pair and re-bands no Phase 04
  obligation.** `21 CFR 11.10(c)` and `21 CFR 11.10(e)` are quoted at `§11` to state what they require,
  **not to assert that Helix meets or fails either** — `ADR-0049`.
- **It states no validation outcome.** The evidence Phase 04's framework requires is owed in full —
  `ADR-0034`, `IS-34`.
- **It raises no CAPA and no deviation, and states no remediation plan, cost, sequence or priority.**
- **It disposes of neither `OBS-2` nor `OBS-4`** — Phase 08.
- **It characterises no reintegration** — `ADR-0043`.
- **It reaches no legal conclusion of any kind**, characterises nothing as a violation of anything, and
  forecasts no inspection outcome. **A Form FDA 483 is not a final agency determination.**
- **Nothing here bears on `IS-01`**, which stays open and stays unweighted, and **no candidate explanation
  of it is promoted by statement, by ordering or by tone** — `ADR-0012`.
