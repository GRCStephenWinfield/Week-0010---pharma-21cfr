## 1. What this chapter settles

This is the phase that publishes a validation framework, and the first thing it has to say is that no
regulation asked for one.

**No FDA regulation and no FDA guidance requires a validation framework, a validation master plan or a
validation policy.** None of those three phrases appears in 21 CFR Parts 210, 211 or 11. None appears in
FDA's December 2018 drug CGMP data integrity guidance. A reader who has been told that a validation master
plan is a CGMP requirement has been told it by somebody who did not go and look.

**What the regulations do require is written procedures**, and they require them in three separate places
with three different objects. This chapter quotes all three in full, because a negative claim of this size
is only worth making if the positive claim it sits next to can be checked word by word.

Phase 03 opened with the same shape of statement about the data integrity risk assessment and
`../03-data-integrity-risk-assessment-and-the-alcoa-attributes/03.01-nothing-required-this.md` owns that
argument. This chapter is not a restatement of it. Phase 03's negative was about an **assessment**; this
one is about an **instrument that tells Helix what evidence to produce**, and the two are different
artefacts answering to different provisions.

**This chapter states no result.** It does not say how many obligations the framework produced, what
Helix's procedure estate turned out to contain, or what any validation package does or does not address.
`../04.06-the-obligations.md`, `../04.07-the-packages-helix-already-holds.md` and
`../04.08-where-the-obligations-land.md` own those, and this chapter cites them rather than anticipating
them.

## 2. The three provisions that do require something in writing

Each of the three is quoted in full. Each has a different object, and reading any one of them as the
general validation-documentation requirement is the error this section exists to prevent.

### 2.1 `21 CFR 211.100(a)` — written procedures for production and process control

> *"There shall be written procedures for production and process control designed to assure that the drug
> products have the identity, strength, quality, and purity they purport or are represented to possess.
> Such procedures shall include all requirements in this subpart. These written procedures, including any
> changes, shall be drafted, reviewed, and approved by the appropriate organizational units and reviewed
> and approved by the quality control unit."*

**Three obligations sit in that paragraph and only the first is usually quoted.** There shall be written
procedures; the procedures shall include all requirements in the subpart; and the procedures, **including
any changes**, shall be drafted, reviewed and approved by the appropriate organisational units **and**
reviewed and approved by the quality control unit. **The last clause is the one that reaches this phase**,
because it means a procedure Helix writes to discharge an obligation is not a procedure until the quality
control unit has reviewed and approved it, and a change to one is subject to the same route.

Its object is **production and process control**. It is not, by its terms, a requirement about
computerised systems, and this phase does not cite it as one.

### 2.2 `21 CFR 211.22(d)` — the quality control unit's own procedures, and the sting

> *"The responsibilities and procedures applicable to the quality control unit shall be in writing; such
> written procedures shall be followed."*

**Two clauses. In writing, and followed.** The provision requires no framework, no policy and no plan. It
requires that whatever the quality control unit's procedures are, they exist on paper and are then
actually applied.

**That provision is the reason this phase exists and it is also the largest single exposure this phase
creates**, and `../04.11-what-a-framework-cannot-fix.md §4` owns the second half of that sentence. Phase 03
met the same clause from the other side when it published a scale and recorded, at `IS-19`, that writing
one makes it enforceable against Helix. **The framework is a larger written procedure than the scale was.**

### 2.3 `21 CFR 211.68(a)` — the written program designed to assure proper performance

> *"Automatic, mechanical, or electronic equipment or other types of equipment, including computers, or
> related systems that will perform a function satisfactorily, may be used in the manufacture, processing,
> packing, and holding of a drug product. If such equipment is so used, it shall be routinely calibrated,
> inspected, or checked according to a written program designed to assure proper performance. Written
> records of those calibration checks and inspections shall be maintained."*

**Read the second sentence to its end before deciding what it requires.** The written program is a program
of routine calibration, inspection or checking, and its stated design purpose is to assure proper
performance. It is a requirement that a written program exist and that equipment be calibrated, inspected
or checked according to it — not a requirement that a firm hold a document called a validation master plan,
and not a general licence to describe any validation documentation as required by `21 CFR 211.68(a)`.

**The word *validation* does not appear in `21 CFR 211.68(a)`.** Its only appearance anywhere in
`21 CFR 211.68` is a narrow conditional in `(b)`: where a backup file is not maintained because certain
data are eliminated by computerisation, *"In such instances a written record of the program shall be
maintained along with appropriate validation data."* The repository's standing form of words is that `21 CFR 211.68` requires
**appropriate controls over** Helix's computerised systems — and, at `(a)`, a written program designed to
assure their proper performance. This phase keeps that form and does not enlarge it.

## 3. The negative, stated at the level of the source

| Source | What it says about a validation framework, a validation master plan or a validation policy |
|---|---|
| **21 CFR Part 210** | None of the three phrases appears. Part 210 states the status of the CGMP regulations and defines terms |
| **21 CFR Part 211** | None of the three phrases appears. Part 211 requires **written procedures** in a number of places, each with its own object, and `21 CFR 211.68(a)` requires a **written program** for routine calibration, inspection or checking. **It requires no document that governs how validation is planned across an estate** |
| **21 CFR Part 11** | `21 CFR 11.10(a)` requires **validation of systems**. It does not require a plan, a policy, a framework or a document of any kind describing how validation will be approached. `21 CFR 11.10(j)` requires **written policies** — of a specific and different kind, holding individuals accountable and responsible for actions initiated under their electronic signatures — and it is not a validation-planning requirement |
| **FDA, December 2018, *Data Integrity and Compliance With Drug CGMP: Questions and Answers*** | Addresses the **extent** of validation studies at **Q3** — *"The extent of validation studies should be commensurate with the risk posed by the automated system."* A nonbinding *should*, about extent. **It names no artefact** |
| **FDA, August 2003, *Part 11, Electronic Records; Electronic Signatures — Scope and Application*** | Recommends that firms base their approach on **a justified and documented risk assessment**. **Nonbinding**, and again no artefact is named |

**Two consequences follow and this phase holds both.**

**The framework is Helix's instrument and not a compliance artefact Helix was told to hold.** It was
authorised as a programme decision at `GOV-16`, and it could have been designed differently or not built at
all. No chapter of this phase writes *FDA requires* about the framework, about the policy or about any of
the procedures the policy architecture specifies.

**And the guidance that does discuss validation extent is nonbinding, and this phase says so at every
citation.** FDA's own words on the face of the December 2018 guidance govern the reading of everything
quoted from it:

> *"In general, FDA's guidance documents do not establish legally enforceable responsibilities. Instead,
> guidances describe the Agency's current thinking on a topic and should be viewed only as
> recommendations, unless specific regulatory or statutory requirements are cited. The use of the word
> should in Agency guidances means that something is suggested or recommended, but not required."*

## 4. What is required is validation, and that survives everything

The negative in `§3` is about the **instrument**. It is not a statement that validation is optional, and a
reader who takes it that way has read the opposite of what this chapter says.

**`21 CFR 11.10(a)` requires validation of systems**, and `../04.03-the-fourth-clause.md` quotes it whole
and owns what its four clauses ask. FDA has stated an intention to exercise enforcement discretion over
`21 CFR 11.10(a)` and over the corresponding requirements in `21 CFR 11.30`. **Enforcement discretion is
FDA's stated intention. It is not a legal exemption, it is not a safe harbour and it confers no rights** —
`ADR-0003`, argued at `01.04 §3`. A programme that reads enforcement discretion as permission has read the
wrong document.

**And the predicate rule obligation survives it entirely, in FDA's own words** — the August 2003 guidance
at `§III.C.1`, quoted whole because the subordinate clause on its own says the opposite of the sentence:

> *"Although persons must still comply with all applicable predicate rule requirements for validation
> (e.g., 21 CFR 820.70(i)), this guidance should not be read to impose any additional requirements for
> validation."*

**The illustrative citation FDA gives is a device provision. For Helix the surviving hook is
`21 CFR 211.68`** — which is a regulation and not a guidance, and FDA has announced no discretion over it.
So the position Helix is actually in is this: it must
exercise appropriate controls over its computerised systems and operate a written program designed to
assure their proper performance, it must produce appropriate validation data where `21 CFR 211.68(b)`'s
backup conditional bites, and `21 CFR 11.10(a)` requires validation of the systems that hold its Part 11
records. **None of that is discharged by holding a framework, and none of it is excused by not holding
one.**

## 5. So why did Helix build one

Four reasons, in the order of their weight, and every one of them is Helix's.

**One — because sixty-one systems cannot be validated one argument at a time.** Phase 02 determined that
sixty-one systems hold at least one Part 11 record. Each of those systems could be approached on its own
merits, by whoever happened to be in the room, and the result would be sixty-one defensible decisions that
nobody could compare. **The framework is how Helix makes the same decision the same way twice**, and it is
the only thing in the phase that makes a validation plan for one system reviewable against a validation
plan for another.

**Two — because Phase 03 produced an input that needs somewhere to land.** Phase 03 handed forward one
hundred and twenty-one record-type/system pairs, seventy-four of them scored and forty-seven of them
deliberately not. `IS-21` records what was missing: **the assessment says where the data is weak, and
nothing in the quality system yet says what Helix must do about it.** A register nobody is required to act
on is a description. **The framework is where a determination acquires a consequence**, and
`../04.02-what-the-framework-has-to-answer.md` sets out exactly what it was handed.

**Three — because `21 CFR 211.68(b)` graduates a control and Helix has to be able to say on what basis.**
The provision requires that the degree and frequency of input/output verification **be based on** the
complexity and reliability of the computer or related system. It does not say how a firm is to know what
those are, and Phase 03 recorded at `DP-15` that neither axis of its scale is complexity or reliability.
**`../04.04-two-graduations.md` is the answer to that dependency, and it is the reason the framework has
two graduations rather than one.**

**Four — because the quality control unit has to be able to say, in writing, how it decided.**
`21 CFR 211.22(d)` requires its responsibilities and procedures to be in writing and provides that *"such
written procedures shall be followed."* That cuts both ways, and this phase says so out loud rather than
enjoying only the half that flatters it. It is the reason to write the framework down, and it is the reason
to keep the framework small.

## 6. `ADR-0023` — the framework says so in its own first section

**The framework document's own first section states that nothing required it to exist.** That is
`ADR-0023`, taken at `DEC-403` on 2026-10-09 by Colm Ó Braonáin, and it is a requirement on the artefact
rather than a sentiment about it.

The reason is narrow and it is worth stating plainly. **An instrument that presents itself as a requirement
invites a reader to stop asking what actually is one.** A validation framework whose opening page reads as
though FDA demanded it produces a reader who believes the framework is the obligation — and a reader who
believes that will discharge the framework and think the regulation is satisfied. The regulation is
`21 CFR 11.10(a)` and `21 CFR 211.68`, and those are the things a determination has to be defensible
against. **Helix's framework is a means. Saying so on its first page is how the means is kept from being
mistaken for the end.**

The same discipline is applied elsewhere in the phase to two documents that genuinely are not requirements
and are regularly cited as though they were. **FDA's Computer Software Assurance guidance is device
guidance — issued by CDRH and CBER, anchored on `21 CFR Part 820` as amended by the Quality Management
System Regulation — and it does not apply to Helix; it is adopted voluntarily and labelled as voluntary at
every citation.** **GAMP 5 Second Edition, ISPE, July 2022, is good-practice guidance and never a
requirement.** `ADR-0026` fixes the labelling rule for both, `ADR-0005` fixed it in Phase 01 for the first,
and `../04.05-the-framework.md §8` says where each is used and what it is used for.

## 8. What this chapter does not do

- **It does not describe the framework.** `../04.05-the-framework.md` owns the instrument, its parts and
  the evidence it requires for each paragraph of `21 CFR 11.10`.
- **It does not set out what `21 CFR 11.10(a)` asks.** `../04.03-the-fourth-clause.md` owns the four
  clauses and quotes the paragraph whole.
- **It does not describe the two graduations.** `../04.04-two-graduations.md`.
- **It states no result of any kind** — no count of obligations, no count of procedures, no count of
  validation packages, no rigour outcome for any system. Those are `../04.06-the-obligations.md` to
  `../04.09-the-policy-architecture.md`.
- **It states no validation outcome.** No system is validated in this phase, no protocol is run and no test
  result exists.
- **It states no control position.** Whether any paragraph of `21 CFR 11.10` is met, for any system, is
  Phase 05's — `ADR-0029`, `IS-20`, `IS-09`.
- **It reaches no legal conclusion**, characterises nothing as a violation of anything, and forecasts no
  inspection outcome.
