# 02.07 — Open and Closed

*Phase 02 speaks as at **2026-07-31**. Helix Pharma, its systems and its findings are fictional and
illustrative. **21 CFR Part 11 is real**, and every passage quoted below is quoted from the regulation's
own text.*

---

## 1. The determination

**All sixty-one systems that hold at least one Part 11 record are closed systems.**

**Three of them had been classified open, and the classification rested on the wrong test.** Helix's IT
standard called the three vendor-hosted systems open because they sit outside the site firewall. The
regulation does not ask where a system sits. **It asks who controls access**, Helix administers every
account on all three, and on the test the regulation states, all three are closed.

The sixty-one are `02.08-the-sixty-one-and-the-thirty-five.md`'s and the record types that put each of
them in scope are `02.05-the-fifty-eight-record-types.md`'s. This chapter takes that population as given
and does one thing with it: applies `21 CFR 11.3(b)(4)` and `21 CFR 11.3(b)(9)`, to all sixty-one, on the
evidence.

**The chapter is written the long way round on purpose.** The right answer here is not the intuitive one,
and a chapter that presented it as obvious would be no use to a reader who currently believes the
intuitive one — which is most readers, because the intuitive one is what the industry says.

## 2. The regulation, quoted

**`21 CFR 11.3(b)(4)` — closed system**, verbatim:

> *"Closed system means an environment in which system access is controlled by persons who are
> responsible for the content of electronic records that are on the system."*

**`21 CFR 11.3(b)(9)` — open system**, verbatim:

> *"Open system means an environment in which system access is not controlled by persons who are
> responsible for the content of electronic records that are on the system."*

**The two definitions differ by one word.** Everything else in them is identical. The distinction the
regulation draws is not between kinds of infrastructure, kinds of network or kinds of contract. It is
between **an environment where the people responsible for the content of the records control who gets
into it**, and one where they do not.

Three things follow directly from the text, and each of them is a thing the industry routinely gets
wrong.

**"Access is controlled by persons who are responsible for the content."** Not by an organisation in the
abstract, and not by whoever owns the hardware. The test asks whether the control of access and the
responsibility for the record content sit with the same persons.

**Nothing in either definition mentions a network, a firewall, a hosting model, the internet or a
physical location.** `21 CFR 11.3(b)` contains **nine definitions and no more** — *Act*, *Agency*,
*Biometrics*, *Closed system*, *Digital signature*, *Electronic record*, *Electronic signature*,
*Handwritten signature*, *Open system* — and none of them is a definition of a network boundary.

**The definitions are exhaustive of the question.** A system is closed or it is open, and it is one of the
two because of who controls access to it. There is no third state, no partially open state, and no state
that depends on how uncomfortable the arrangement makes anybody feel.

`01.03 §4` fixed this test in Phase 01, before Helix had gathered a single fact about any system, and
recorded expressly that Phase 01 made **no determination** about the vendor-hosted electronic quality
management system. That sequencing matters: the test was written down while nobody knew which answer it
would produce.

## 3. What Helix's IT standard said

**Helix's IT standard classified a system as open if it sat outside the site firewall, and closed if it
sat inside.** On that rule, the three vendor-hosted systems were open and everything else was closed. The
rule was applied consistently, it was written down, and it was easy to operate — anybody could answer it
from a network diagram.

**It was also not the test the regulation states**, and the ways it goes wrong run in both directions.

| The network-position rule says | The regulation asks | Where they part company |
|---|---|---|
| Outside the firewall, therefore open | Who provisions, grants, revokes and reviews access? | A vendor-hosted system whose accounts are administered entirely by the regulated entity **is closed**, and calling it open substitutes a fact about routing for a fact about governance |
| Inside the firewall, therefore closed | The same question | An on-premise system whose administration has been handed to a third party who also holds the credentials **is not automatically closed**, and the firewall says nothing about it |
| The boundary is a technical control | The boundary is **responsibility for record content plus control of access** | A perimeter control is a real control. It answers a security question, and `21 CFR 11.3(b)(4)` is not asking a security question |

**The standard was not written in bad faith and nobody at Helix invented the error.** "Hosted means open"
is the single most repeated proposition in this field, and it is repeated by people who are otherwise
careful. It survives because it is easy, because it feels prudent, and because getting it wrong in that
direction looks conservative — a company that treats a closed system as open appears to be doing more,
not less.

**`§7` is where that appearance breaks down**, because doing more of one thing turned out to mean never
having asked about another.

## 4. The test as applied

**Six questions were asked of every one of the sixty-one systems**, and the answers were evidenced rather
than asserted. The questions come out of the words *"access is controlled by"*, and each is a thing a
person can do or not do.

| # | The question | Why it is the question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Who creates an account** on this system, and on whose authorisation? | Provisioning is the first act of controlling access |
| 2 | **Who assigns the role or privilege level**, and who approves it? | Access that cannot be limited by role is not controlled in any useful sense — compare `21 CFR 11.10(d)` and `21 CFR 11.10(g)` |
| 3 | **Who removes an account** when a person leaves or changes role, and how quickly? | The ability to revoke is the other half of control |
| 4 | **Who reviews the account list**, and against what? | Control that is never verified is a claim |
| 5 | **Who holds the administrative credentials**, and can anybody outside Helix use them without Helix's authorisation? | An administrator outside the responsible persons is an access route outside their control |
| 6 | **Are the persons who control access the persons responsible for the content of the records?** | This is the regulation's own words, and questions 1 to 5 exist to answer it |

**The evidence** for each system: the account administration procedure that governs it; the access request
and approval records; the current account list with roles; the leaver process evidence; the record of who
holds administrative credentials; and, for the hosted systems, the contract terms governing the vendor's
own administrative access, together with the vendor's description of how that access is controlled and
logged. The items are indexed in `logs/evidence-index.md` and the determination for each system is carried
on its entry in `logs/systems-register.md`.

**One evidentiary limit is stated rather than glossed.** For the hosted systems, part of the evidence is
the vendor's description of its own administrative arrangements, which Helix has read but has not audited.
**A vendor supplies features that support compliance; it does not supply compliance**, there is no Part 11
certification and no FDA-approved or FDA-validated software, and a vendor statement is a statement.
Qualification of the hosted arrangement is programme work that has not been performed at this vantage, and
`02.12-what-the-determination-costs.md` records it as work rather than as sentiment. The assumption that
the arrangement holds as described is carried in `logs/raid-log.md`.

## 5. The three vendor-hosted systems

| The system | What it does | Who administers access | Determination |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The electronic quality management system** — `SYS-041`, supplied and hosted by **Brightpath QMS, Inc.** | Deviations, CAPA, change control, document control and training records | **Helix.** Every account is created, assigned, reviewed and removed by Helix's own administrators, on authorisation from the record-owning function | **Closed** — `21 CFR 11.3(b)(4)` |
| **The analytical instrument calibration management system** — `SYS-040`, vendor-hosted | The calibration programme and the calibration records of the analytical instruments in both QC laboratories | **Helix.** Same arrangement, administered by Quality Control and Metrology under the same procedure | **Closed** |
| **The serialisation repository and reporting system** — `SYS-056`, vendor-hosted | The serialisation and product identifier data, and the reporting drawn from it | **Helix.** Same arrangement, administered by Supply Chain with Automation | **Closed** |

**In all three, the vendor's own administrative access exists**, as it does for any hosted service. In all
three it is **described by the vendor** as bounded by contract, exercised on Helix's authorisation for
defined support purposes and logged. **Helix has read that description and has not audited it** — `§4`,
and the assumption is carried at `AS-15`. **That is not the same thing as the vendor controlling access**,
and the determination records the
distinction rather than smoothing over it: if the vendor could provision an account into the production
environment at its own discretion, question 5 would be answered differently and so would the
determination.

**Helix administers every account on all three. The persons who control access are the persons responsible
for the content of the records. On `21 CFR 11.3(b)(4)`, all three are closed systems.**

**`ADR-0011` records the decision**, taken on **2026-07-14 by Joachim Reuter** and logged at **`DEC-212`**.
The determination for all sixty-one was approved at **`GOV-09`** on **2026-07-24**, alongside the record-level
applicability determination it depends on.

## 6. All sixty-one, and one distinction that has to be kept

**Sixty-one systems, four administration routes, one determination.**

| Administration route | What it looks like | Determination |
|---|---|---|
| Central IT administration under the site directory | Accounts created and removed by IT on authorisation from the record-owning function | **Closed** |
| Local administration by the using function | A laboratory or production administrator holds the rights, under the same procedure | **Closed** |
| Instrument-embedded administration | Accounts held in the instrument's own application, administered locally | **Closed** |
| Vendor-hosted, administered by Helix | `§5` | **Closed** |

**Not one of the sixty-one is an open system.** That is a real determination and not a formality: if
account administration for any of them had sat with a party that is not responsible for the record
content, the answer would have been different for that system, and the determination would have said so.

**And here is the distinction a reader must not lose.** `OBS-4` on the Form FDA 483 records that a shared
user account was in use on a packaging line system, so entries could not be attributed to an individual.

**A shared account does not make a system open.** Access to that system is still controlled by Helix's own
people. What a shared account defeats is **attribution** — the ability to say which individual did what —
which is a different requirement arising from different text: `21 CFR 211.188(b)(11)` and
`21 CFR 211.194(a)(7)` and `(a)(8)` on the predicate-rule side, and `21 CFR 11.10(d)` and `21 CFR 11.200`
on the Part 11 side.

**This chapter records that distinction and draws no conclusion from it.** The subject matter of `OBS-4`
is not this chapter's, no assessment of any system against `21 CFR 11.10` has been performed, and **a Form
FDA 483 is not a legal finding and not a final agency determination.** Phase 05 is where access and
attribution are examined.

## 7. What the misclassification actually cost, which is not what a reader expects

**The measures Helix deployed for the three hosted systems are not wrong, and this determination removes
none of them.**

Having classified the three as open, Helix did what `21 CFR 11.30` describes for open systems: it deployed
document encryption and digital-signature measures. **Those measures are in place and are not wrong, and
nothing in this phase turns them off. Whether they perform as intended has not been assessed** — no
control has been assessed in this phase — **and their retention is Helix's decision rather than a finding
about their effectiveness.** A company that had classified a system as open and then done nothing about it
would have a worse problem than Helix has.

**What was wrong is what the measures were recorded as discharging.**

**`21 CFR 11.30`**, verbatim:

> *"Persons who use open systems to create, modify, maintain, or transmit electronic records shall employ
> procedures and controls designed to ensure the authenticity, integrity, and, as appropriate, the
> confidentiality of electronic records from the point of their creation to the point of their receipt.
> Such procedures and controls shall include those identified in § 11.10, as appropriate, and additional
> measures such as document encryption and use of appropriate digital signature standards to ensure, as
> necessary under the circumstances, record authenticity, integrity, and confidentiality."*

**Read the middle clause.** *"Such procedures and controls shall include those identified in § 11.10, as
appropriate, and additional measures…"*

**`21 CFR 11.30` requires open-system users to employ procedures and controls that include those
identified in `21 CFR 11.10`, as appropriate, *and* additional measures. The additional measures are
additive to the `§11.10` controls and never a substitute for them**; recording encryption and digital
signatures as the whole of a compliance position leaves the `§11.10` controls unaddressed. **The "as
appropriate" qualifier is the regulation's and is not dropped here**: `21 CFR 11.10` by its own opening
words binds persons who use closed systems, and for an open system its controls apply through
`21 CFR 11.30` and qualified in that way. Encryption and digital signatures are named in the text as
examples of the *additional* measures — they are not a substitute for validation, for limiting system
access to authorised individuals, for audit trails, for authority checks, or for any of the eleven
paragraphs at `21 CFR 11.10(a)` to `(k)`.

**Helix's records described the encryption and digital-signature measures as its compliance position for
those three systems.** They were the answer given when the question was asked. And because they were
recorded as the answer, **the eleven paragraphs of `21 CFR 11.10` were never assessed for those three
systems at all** — not the validation requirement at `21 CFR 11.10(a)`, not the audit trail requirement at
`21 CFR 11.10(e)`, not the access limitation at `21 CFR 11.10(d)`, none of it.

**`IS-09` is that gap**, and it is the finding this chapter hands forward:

> **The three vendor-hosted systems have never been assessed against `21 CFR 11.10`. Their classification
> is corrected; their position under `21 CFR 11.10` is unknown and has not been examined.**

Two things `IS-09` is not.

**It is not a statement that the three systems fail anything.** Nothing has been assessed. An unassessed
position is unknown, not adverse, and this repository does not convert one into the other.

**It is not a reason to remove the encryption or the digital signatures.** They are not required for a
closed system — `21 CFR 11.70` sets the standard for signature-record linking as preventing transfer *"by
ordinary means"* and **mandates no cryptographic hashing**, and digital signatures appear in `21 CFR
11.30` as an example of an additional measure rather than as an obligation anywhere. **Retaining them is
Helix's decision**, taken because a control that works is not improved by being switched off, and it is
recorded as Helix's decision rather than as a requirement.

## 8. What would make a system at Helix open

The determination is a statement about an arrangement, and arrangements change. Four changes would put a
system back into `21 CFR 11.3(b)(9)`, and each is written into the review trigger at
`02.09-the-systems-register.md §5` under `ADR-0015`.

| Change | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Account administration for a system moves to a party that is not responsible for the content of its records** | The persons controlling access and the persons responsible for content stop being the same persons — the definition's own words |
| **A vendor acquires the ability to provision or elevate access in the production environment at its own discretion** | Question 5 at `§4` is answered differently |
| **A system is opened to a population Helix does not administer** — a partner, a contract laboratory, a customer | Access control passes outside the responsible persons for that population |
| **A record type moves onto a system with a different administration arrangement** | The system inherits its scope from the records it holds — `ADR-0001`, `ADR-0013` — and the classification follows the records |

**If any of the four occurs, the affected system is reclassified, and `21 CFR 11.30`'s additional measures
apply in addition to the `21 CFR 11.10` controls it requires be included as appropriate**, in that order
and never the other way round. The lesson
of `§7` is written into the trigger so that the next person to apply it inherits the correction rather
than the error.

## 9. What this chapter does not do

- **It does not assess any system against `21 CFR 11.10`.** It determines which section applies. Whether
  any of the sixty-one satisfies any paragraph of it is unexamined, and Phases 04 and 05 do that work.
- **It does not state that any system is validated or is not validated.** No validation framework exists.
- **It scores nothing.** The data integrity risk assessment is Phase 03's.
- **It does not determine which records are Part 11 records.** `02.05` enumerates the record types and
  `02.08` places the systems; this chapter classifies the systems that population produced.
- **It draws no conclusion about `OBS-4`**, converts no inspectional observation into a deviation or a
  finding, and characterises nothing as a violation of anything. **A Form FDA 483 is not a legal
  finding.**
- **It makes no claim about any vendor's compliance.** There is no Part 11 certification, no
  FDA-approved software and no FDA-validated software. A vendor supplies **features that support**
  compliance.
- **It reaches no legal conclusion of any kind** and forecasts nothing about any future inspection.
