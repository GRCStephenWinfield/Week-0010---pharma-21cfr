*Phase 02 speaks as at **2026-07-31**. Helix Pharma, its estate and its numbers are fictional and
illustrative. **21 CFR 211.68 and 21 CFR Part 11 are real** and are quoted from their own text.*

---

## 1. The question an inventory has to be able to answer

A computerised system inventory is not a catalogue. It is the instrument by which a regulated
manufacturer can answer, in front of somebody entitled to ask, a question with only one acceptable
answer:

**Which computerised systems does this company operate, and for each one, what is it doing in a
regulated process?**

Everything else an inventory is used for — support planning, licence renewal, refresh budgeting,
capacity — is real work, and none of it requires the answer to be complete. **This one does.** A support
list that misses a system produces an unhappy user. A determination built on an inventory that misses a
system produces a statement about the estate that is not true, made in writing, by a company that
believed it.

That is the whole reason this chapter exists before the numbers do. **The count is
`02.02-the-inventory-corrected.md`'s and this chapter does not state it.** What this chapter fixes is
what the thing being counted is for, and what counting rules produced the entries — because a count with
no stated counting rule is a number nobody can check.

## 2. A register lists what the company owns. An inventory supports a determination

**This is the distinction the phase turns on, and it is the distinction Helix did not have.**

| | **A register** | **An inventory that supports a determination** |
|---|---|---|
| Answers | *What do we own, and who supports it?* | *What are we running, and what is each one doing in a regulated process?* |
| Unit | The purchasable, supportable, licensable thing | The system as a functional whole, however it was bought |
| Completeness standard | Complete enough that support and renewal work | **Complete over the population that could hold a record a predicate rule requires** |
| Source of truth | The transaction that created the entry — a purchase, a project, a ticket | **The estate itself, observed** |
| Attributes it must carry | Cost centre, support tier, contract, refresh date | Owner as a named individual, location, function, **what records it holds**, and the determinations derived from them |
| Failure mode | An entry goes stale and somebody is inconvenienced | **A determination is made about a population that is not the population** |
| What its absence looks like | Support surprises | **A statement about scope with nothing under it** |

Both are legitimate documents. They are not the same document, and one cannot be promoted into the other
by relabelling it. **A register becomes an inventory when it is reconciled to the estate and carries the
attributes a determination needs** — and until then it is a register with a new title page.

**Helix had a register.** It was maintained, it was current for its own purpose, and the people who kept
it did the job they had been given. It was not built to support a Part 11 applicability determination
because nobody had ever asked it to, and this chapter does not treat that as a failure of the people who
kept it. It treats it as a fact about what the document was for.

## 3. Where the obligation comes from, stated exactly

The obligation to know what the estate is does not come from Part 11. It comes from the predicate rule,
and it would exist if Part 11 had never been written.

**`21 CFR 211.68(a)`**, in its own words:

> *"Automatic, mechanical, or electronic equipment or other types of equipment, including computers, or
> related systems that will perform a function satisfactorily, may be used in the manufacture,
> processing, packing, and holding of a drug product. If such equipment is so used, it shall be routinely
> calibrated, inspected, or checked according to a written program designed to assure proper performance.
> Written records of those calibration checks and inspections shall be maintained."*

**`21 CFR 211.68(b)`** adds the control obligation:

> *"Appropriate controls shall be exercised over computer or related systems to assure that changes in
> master production and control records or other records are instituted only by authorized personnel."*

Read those two together and the inventory falls out of them. **A written programme covering equipment
used in the manufacture, processing, packing and holding of a drug product presupposes a statement of
what that equipment is.** Appropriate controls exercised over computer or related systems presuppose a
statement of what those systems are. Neither section uses the word *inventory*, and neither has to: an
obligation that runs over a population is unperformable without a statement of the population.

**`21 CFR 211.68` is also the reason none of this waits on Part 11.** Enforcement discretion is FDA's
stated intention about how it will enforce specific Part 11 requirements; it is not an exemption, it
confers no rights, and it does not touch the predicate rule at all. `01.04 §3` owns that argument and
`ADR-0003` records the decision the programme took on it.

Part 11 enters one step later. `21 CFR 11.1(b)` provides that the part *"applies to records in electronic
form that are created, modified, maintained, archived, retrieved, or transmitted, under any records
requirements set forth in agency regulations."* **Records.** Which means that the inventory is a
necessary input to the Part 11 determination and is not the determination — the determination is made
record type by record type under `ADR-0001`, and `02.04-the-record-type-as-the-unit-of-determination.md`
owns that argument.

## 4. What Helix's IT register was for instead

The register was created and maintained by IT and Automation as an asset and support record. Its entries
were created by the events that create asset records: a purchase order, a project handover, a support
contract, a licence renewal. It carried what those events supply.

| The register carried | The register did not carry |
|---|---|
| An entry name and a supporting team | A named individual owner accountable for the system |
| A support tier and a contract reference | A statement of what the system does in a regulated process |
| A licence position and a renewal date | Any attribute identifying whether the system holds records required by a predicate rule |
| A hardware or hosting reference | Any statement of the record types held |
| A refresh or end-of-life date | Any determination of Part 11 applicability, because none existed to record |

**It was accurate about the things it was built to be accurate about.** Two properties made it unusable
as the basis of a determination, and neither is a defect in an asset register.

**Its entries were created by transactions, not by observation.** A system that arrived without a
transaction IT recognised — bought on a capital equipment line with the instrument it controls, installed
by an equipment vendor during commissioning, or built on a workstation that was itself the registered
asset — had no event to create an entry. Nothing in the register's design would notice.

**Nothing in its lifecycle removed an entry.** Decommissioning is a project event and a register entry
survives it unless somebody closes the entry deliberately. A register that is never reconciled to the
estate accumulates entries in one direction and loses systems in the other, and both errors are invisible
from inside the document.

## 5. The walk, and what it covered

**`GOV-06`, dated 2026-06-05, authorised a physical walk of the estate and fixed its method before it
began.** The method was fixed first deliberately: a walk whose rules are written after the walk is a
walk whose rules were chosen by what it found.

| Location | What was covered | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Ridgemont, New Jersey** | Manufacturing and packaging halls, the warehouse, the primary QC laboratory, the documentation office, engineering and maintenance areas, and the utility and plant rooms serving GxP areas | Room by room, with the area's own supervisor present |
| **Cary, North Carolina** | The second QC laboratory — stability and release testing | Room by room, with the laboratory's own supervisor present |
| **The two on-premise data centres at Ridgemont** | Rack by rack, against the hosting records | Physical inspection with Automation and Infrastructure present |
| **The co-located disaster-recovery site** | The systems replicated to it, and any system running there | Physical inspection, with the replication configuration read alongside |
| **The three vendor-hosted systems** | Reached by documentation rather than by walking — the contract, the hosting description, and **the account administration arrangement** | Desk review with the system's Helix owner, evidenced as `EV` items in `logs/evidence-index.md` |

**Princeton was not walked and is not in the inventory.** `01.01 §3` records that no manufacturing, no
testing, no batch record, no release decision and no record required to be maintained under a predicate
rule is created or held at the corporate office. That determination is Phase 01's and this phase does not
reopen it; it records that the walk relied on it.

**The walk was performed by the programme with the area supervisors, not by IT alone.** A walk performed
by the function that maintains the register will find what the register describes. The cooperation the walk
depended on is carried as a dependency in `logs/raid-log.md`, and `ADR-0002`'s reporting line — the
Data Integrity Lead reports to Quality and not to IT — is the reason the programme could ask for a walk of
IT's own estate and get one.

## 6. How a system was counted

**Seven counting rules, fixed in `GOV-06` before the walk and applied without exception.** The rules
matter more than they look: most of the difference between one honest count of an estate and another
honest count of the same estate is a difference of counting rule, not of observation.

| # | Rule | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **A computerised system is a set of software and hardware components that together fulfil a function.** It is counted once, as that function | Counting servers counts infrastructure; counting functions counts systems |
| 2 | **A system is counted once regardless of how many servers, nodes or instances it runs on.** A clustered pair is one system; a system with a test environment is one system with an attribute | Otherwise the count measures the hosting design |
| 3 | **An instrument with its own controller, its own user administration and its own local storage of results is a system in its own right** | It creates and holds records independently of anything else, whatever budget bought it |
| 4 | **An instrument that stores nothing and writes only into a controlling application is counted with that application**, and the instrument is named in the entry | The records live in one place and the entry says where |
| 5 | **Hosting does not change the count and does not change the entry's status.** A vendor-hosted system is a system | `21 CFR 11.3(b)(4)` turns on who controls access, not on where the servers are — `02.07-open-and-closed.md` |
| 6 | **A system that is present but not in service is recorded and marked**, not silently omitted | A powered-down system with retained data is a records question, not an absence |
| 7 | **A register entry with no system behind it is removed**, and the removal is recorded with its evidence | An inventory that keeps entries to avoid explaining their removal is a register again |

Two consequences of those rules are worth stating plainly, because a reader is entitled to know which way
the count leans.

**Rule 3 raises the count and rule 4 lowers it.** Applied together they produce a count that follows where
records are created and held, which is the only property the later determination cares about. A rule set
chosen to make the estate look smaller would have merged rule 3 into rule 4; a rule set chosen to make the
programme look thorough would have done the reverse.

**Rule 2 means the inventory is not a hosting document.** It says what runs, not how many boxes it runs
on. `02.09-the-systems-register.md` records what each entry carries, and hosting appears there as an
attribute rather than as an entry.

## 7. What the walk found, and what the walk could not settle

**The result is `02.02-the-inventory-corrected.md`'s and this chapter does not state it**, in either
direction. What belongs here is the limit of the method.

**A walk establishes presence. It does not establish function.** Seeing a system in a room tells you it
exists; what it does in a regulated process is a determination made against evidence, and
`02.03-what-makes-a-system-gxp-relevant.md` owns the test and the evidence for it.

**A walk cannot see a hosted system.** For the three vendor-hosted systems the equivalent of walking the
room is reading who administers the accounts, and that evidence is a contract, an administration record
and a demonstration rather than a rack. `02.07-open-and-closed.md` sets out what was gathered and what it
established.

**A walk is true on the day it is performed.** An estate changes. `ADR-0015` makes the determination a
controlled document with a named owner and a review trigger for exactly that reason, and
`02.09-the-systems-register.md §5` names the triggers. The assumption that no new system entered the estate
between the walk and this vantage is carried in `logs/raid-log.md`.

## 8. What this chapter does not do

- **It states no count.** Not the register's, not the walk's, not the difference between them. `02.02`.
- **It determines nothing about any system.** Not GxP relevance, not Part 11 applicability, not closed or
  open.
- **It states no validation status for any system, and no risk rating of any kind.** Validation is Phase
  04; the data integrity risk assessment is Phase 03; **nothing in this phase is scored.**
- **It makes no statement about whether any system meets any requirement of Part 11**, and it reaches no
  legal conclusion of any kind.
- **It characterises nobody's work.** The register was built for a purpose and served it. The people who
  maintained it were not asked for a determination and did not fail to supply one.
