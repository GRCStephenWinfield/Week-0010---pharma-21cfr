*Phase 01 speaks as at **2026-05-29**. Helix Pharma and its programme are fictional and illustrative.
**21 CFR Part 11 is real**, and everything this chapter says about it is drawn from the regulation's
own text and from FDA's published guidance. Where the regulation is quoted, it is quoted exactly.*

---

## 1. The regulation, at its actual size

**21 CFR Part 11 — Electronic Records; Electronic Signatures.** Promulgated in 1997; eCFR prints the
source as **62 FR 13464**.

**Part 11 contains exactly ten sections, in three subparts.**

| Subpart | Section | Title |
|---|---|---|
| **A — General Provisions** | `21 CFR 11.1` | Scope |
| | `21 CFR 11.2` | Implementation |
| | `21 CFR 11.3` | Definitions |
| **B — Electronic Records** | `21 CFR 11.10` | Controls for closed systems |
| | `21 CFR 11.30` | Controls for open systems |
| | `21 CFR 11.50` | Signature manifestations |
| | `21 CFR 11.70` | Signature/record linking |
| **C — Electronic Signatures** | `21 CFR 11.100` | General requirements |
| | `21 CFR 11.200` | Electronic signature components and controls |
| | `21 CFR 11.300` | Controls for identification codes/passwords |

**There is no §11.60, no §11.400 and no Subpart D.** The gaps in the numbering — 11.3 to 11.10, 11.10
to 11.30, 11.70 to 11.100 — are original to the rule and are not omissions. This programme cites
sections that exist and writes them out in full: **`21 CFR 11.10(e)`, never "Part 11.10"**. A citation
that cannot be looked up is not a citation.

**On the amendment history, the industry claim and the accurate statement differ.** It is commonly said
that Part 11 has never been amended since 1997; that is false as stated, the part having been amended
most recently on **2021-12-03**. What is true, and what this repository says instead, is that **the
amendments are confined to scope exclusions added at `21 CFR 11.1(f)`–`(p)` — which exclude specified
food-safety record sets and touch no drug CGMP record — and an administrative address change at
`21 CFR 11.100(c)(1)`. The substantive control requirements in §§ 11.10, 11.30, 11.50, 11.70, 11.200
and 11.300 read as originally promulgated.**

## 2. Part 11 applies to records, not to systems

**This section owns that argument for the whole repository. It is `ADR-0001`, and every later phase
depends on it.**

The commonest sentence in this field is "system X is a Part 11 system". It is a shorthand, it is
convenient, and it is the source of more scoping error than anything else. **Part 11 does not apply to
systems. It applies to records — and, derivatively, to the systems that hold them.**

`21 CFR 11.1(b)` says so directly: the part *"applies to records in electronic form that are created,
modified, maintained, archived, retrieved, or transmitted, under any records requirements set forth in
agency regulations."* Records. Not applications, not servers, not platforms.

FDA's August 2003 Scope and Application guidance narrows it further. Under the **narrow
interpretation**, Part 11 reaches four categories:

| # | Category of record or signature |
|---|---|
| 1 | Records **required to be maintained under predicate rule requirements** and **maintained in electronic format in place of paper format** |
| 2 | Records required to be maintained under predicate rules, **maintained in electronic format in addition to paper**, and **relied on to perform regulated activities** |
| 3 | Records **submitted to FDA** under predicate rules in electronic format, where the record type has been identified in the relevant docket as one the agency accepts electronically |
| 4 | **Electronic signatures** intended to be the equivalent of handwritten signatures, initials and other general signings **required by predicate rules** |

FDA is equally explicit about the converse: *"records (and any associated signatures) that are not
required to be retained under predicate rules, but that are nonetheless maintained in electronic
format, are not part 11 records."*

**Every one of those four categories starts from a predicate rule.** That is the mechanism. A record is
in Part 11 scope because some other regulation — for Helix, `21 CFR Part 211` — required it to exist,
and because Helix keeps it electronically and relies on it. Part 11 sets the conditions under which
FDA will treat the electronic version as trustworthy. It does not, by itself, require any record to be
created.

Three consequences the programme takes from this, and holds:

**A single system commonly holds records of several classes at once.** The same laboratory application
may hold data that `21 CFR 211.194(a)` requires, configuration that supports it, training material that
no predicate rule requires, and a scheduling calendar. To call the whole application "a Part 11 system"
is to apply a control set to the calendar and, worse, to invite an argument about whether the calendar
needs an audit trail — an argument nobody can win, because the premise is wrong.

**Scope must be determined record by record, and written down.** This repository never says "a Part 11
system" without saying which records make it one. Where the phrase is unavoidable as shorthand, it is
followed immediately by the record class.

**Neither this chapter nor this phase makes any applicability determination.** No record class at Helix
has been assessed and no system has been declared in or out of scope. That work is
`02-the-system-inventory-and-part-11-applicability`; Phase 01 states only the method.

## 3. The nine definitions, and the words Part 11 does not define

**`21 CFR 11.3(b)` contains nine definitions and no more.**

In order: `11.3(b)(1)` *Act* · `(2)` *Agency* · `(3)` *Biometrics* · `(4)` *Closed system* · `(5)`
*Digital signature* · `(6)` *Electronic record* · `(7)` *Electronic signature* · `(8)` *Handwritten
signature* · `(9)` *Open system*.

**Part 11 defines none of the following: "audit trail", "metadata", "data integrity", "static record",
"dynamic record".**

That is not a gap in the reader's knowledge; it is a fact about the regulation, and it is one of the
most useful facts in this phase. Every one of those five terms is in daily use in this industry, and
every one of them is routinely attributed to Part 11 by people who have not opened it. `21 CFR 11.10(e)`
requires the *use of* audit trails and describes their properties without ever naming the thing. The
definitions this industry actually uses for those five terms come from FDA's **December 2018 drug CGMP
data integrity guidance**, and `01.04 §6` cites them there, where they belong.

**Electronic record**, as Part 11 defines it, is deliberately broad: *"any combination of text,
graphics, data, audio, pictorial, or other information representation in digital form that is created,
modified, maintained, archived, retrieved, or distributed by a computer system."* Note what it does
**not** contain — any requirement that the record be important, be GxP, or be required by anything.
The narrowing is done by `11.1(b)` and by the predicate rule, not by the definition.

## 4. Closed and open systems: the test is who controls access

**`21 CFR 11.3(b)(4)` — closed system**, verbatim:

> *"an environment in which system access is controlled by persons who are responsible for the content
> of electronic records that are on the system."*

**`21 CFR 11.3(b)(9)` — open system**, verbatim:

> *"an environment in which system access is not controlled by persons who are responsible for the
> content of electronic records that are on the system."*

**The test is who controls access. It is not where the servers are.**

This is the point the industry most often gets backwards, and it gets it backwards in a way that costs
money. A vendor-hosted system is **not** automatically an open system. An internet-facing system is not
automatically an open system. An air-gapped system is not automatically closed. The question is a
governance question: **do the persons responsible for the content of the records control who gets into
the environment those records sit in?** Where a hosting arrangement leaves account provisioning, role
assignment, privilege grant and revocation in the hands of the regulated entity — and the contract and
the audit rights back that up — the system can be closed on any infrastructure.

The consequence of getting it wrong is not academic. `21 CFR 11.30` requires, for open systems, the
controls of `21 CFR 11.10` *"as appropriate"* **plus** *"additional measures such as document
encryption and use of appropriate digital signature standards."* A company that classifies a hosted
system as open because it is hosted has bought itself an encryption and digital-signature programme it
may not have needed, and has usually still not asked who controls access.

Helix operates a vendor-hosted electronic quality management system supplied by **Brightpath QMS, Inc.**
**This document makes no determination about whether it is closed or open** — that requires facts about
access control, contract terms and administrative privilege which Phase 02 will gather. What this
document fixes is the test.

## 5. `21 CFR 11.10` — the controls for closed systems

Section 11.10 opens with an obligation on **persons**, not on products:

> *"Persons who use closed systems to create, modify, maintain, or transmit electronic records shall
> employ procedures and controls designed to ensure the authenticity, integrity, and, when appropriate,
> the confidentiality of electronic records, and to ensure that the signer cannot readily repudiate the
> signed record as not genuine."*

**Procedures and controls, employed by persons.** That phrasing is why there is no such thing as
Part 11 compliant software: the duty is on the regulated entity's use of its system, and a vendor
cannot discharge it on Helix's behalf.

**`21 CFR 11.10` has eleven paragraphs, (a) through (k).**

`(a)` validation · `(b)` accurate and complete copies · `(c)` protection of records throughout the
retention period · `(d)` limiting system access to authorised individuals · `(e)` audit trails ·
`(f)` operational system checks · `(g)` authority checks · `(h)` device checks · `(i)` education,
training and experience · `(j)` written accountability policies · `(k)` controls over systems
documentation.

### `21 CFR 11.10(a)`, in full, with the clause everybody drops

> *"Validation of systems to ensure accuracy, reliability, consistent intended performance, and the
> ability to discern invalid or altered records."*

**Four clauses. Accuracy. Reliability. Consistent intended performance. And the ability to discern
invalid or altered records.**

The fourth is routinely dropped from industry summaries, and **this portfolio never drops it**, because
it is the clause that turns validation from a functional exercise into a data integrity one. A
validation package that proves a system calculates correctly and behaves consistently has addressed
three of the four. Whether the system lets a user, or an administrator, alter a record in a way that
cannot afterwards be discerned is a separate question, and it is the question the fourth clause asks.

### `21 CFR 11.10(e)`, in full

> *"Use of secure, computer-generated, time-stamped audit trails to independently record the date and
> time of operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete electronic records. Record
> changes shall not obscure previously recorded information. Such audit trail documentation shall be
> retained for a period at least as long as that required for the subject electronic records and shall
> be available for agency review and copying."*

Read what it actually says, clause by clause.

**"Operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete."** Three verbs. **Reading is not among
them.** Part 11 says nothing whatever about recording read or view events.

**"Record changes shall not obscure previously recorded information."** This is a **no-overwrite**
requirement. The prior value must remain discernible. **It is not a reason-for-change requirement** —
there is no textual requirement anywhere in Part 11 for a reason-for-change field, and this repository
never claims one.

**"Retained for a period at least as long as that required for the subject electronic records."** The
audit trail inherits the retention period of the record it describes. It does not have one of its own.

**"Available for agency review and copying."** The obligation runs to FDA. **`21 CFR 11.10(e)` imposes
no internal review obligation at all** — no periodic review, no second-person review, no review at any
frequency. The review obligation for a drug manufacturer arises from the predicate rules, and
`01.04 §7` owns it.

## 6. Subpart C — the signature sections

| Section | What it requires |
|---|---|
| `21 CFR 11.50` | A signed electronic record must clearly indicate **the printed name of the signer**, **the date and time the signature was executed**, and **the meaning associated with the signature** — such as review, approval, responsibility or authorship — and those three must appear **in any human readable form of the record**, including a printout |
| `21 CFR 11.70` | Signatures shall be linked to their records *"to ensure that the signatures cannot be excised, copied, or otherwise transferred to falsify an electronic record **by ordinary means**."* |
| `21 CFR 11.100` | Each electronic signature is **unique to one individual**, never reused or reassigned; the organisation **verifies the identity** of the individual before sanctioning the signature; and the organisation **certifies to FDA** that its electronic signatures are intended to be the legally binding equivalent of handwritten signatures |
| `21 CFR 11.200(a)(1)` | Non-biometric electronic signatures shall *"employ **at least two distinct identification components** such as an identification code and password."* |
| `21 CFR 11.200(a)(1)(i)` | Within *"a single, continuous period of controlled system access"*, subsequent signings may use one component — and that component must be *"only executable by, and designed to be used only by, the individual"* |
| `21 CFR 11.200(b)` | Biometric signatures are a **separate permitted route**. Biometrics are never required |
| `21 CFR 11.300` | Controls over identification codes and passwords: uniqueness of the combination; **periodic checking, recall or revision** of issuances; loss management for tokens and cards; transaction safeguards against unauthorised use, with immediate and urgent reporting of attempts; and initial and periodic testing of devices |

Two of those rows carry traps worth naming.

**`21 CFR 11.200(a)(1)` is not multi-factor authentication.** The text says *"at least two distinct
identification components such as an identification code and password."* An identification code and a
password satisfy it. Modern MFA is a different standard from a different tradition, and the two happen
to overlap without being the same thing. **This repository never describes `21 CFR 11.200` as an MFA
requirement.** Where Helix chooses to deploy MFA, it is Helix's decision, taken for its own reasons.

**The component permitted for subsequent signings is the password, not the user ID.** `21 CFR
11.200(a)(1)(i)` requires that the one component be *"only executable by, and designed to be used only
by, the individual."* A user name is typically visible and is not private. A system that re-prompts for
user name alone within a session has not met the text; one that re-prompts for the password has.

## 7. The paper-printout route, and the three conditions it actually has

The 2003 guidance says, on the printout question:

> *"when persons use computers to generate paper printouts of electronic records, and those paper
> records meet all the requirements of the applicable predicate rules and persons rely on the paper
> records to perform their regulated activities, FDA would generally not consider persons to be 'using
> electronic records in lieu of paper records' under §§ 11.2(a) and 11.2(b)."*

This is regularly summarised as "if you print it, Part 11 does not apply". **The test has three
conditions and they are conjunctive — all three must hold.**

| # | Condition | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The paper record **meets all the requirements of the applicable predicate rules** | Does the printout contain everything Part 211 requires the record to contain? |
| 2 | The person **relies on the paper record** to perform the regulated activity | Is the paper what the reviewer actually reads and signs? |
| 3 | The electronic record is **not also relied on** | Is anybody going back to the system for anything? |

**For a dynamic record, condition 1 generally fails.** A chromatogram is not a picture of a result; it
is a data set a reviewer interacts with — reprocessing an integration, changing baseline parameters,
examining what was integrated and what was excluded. `21 CFR 211.194(a)(4)` requires *"a complete
record of all data secured in the course of each test, including all graphs, charts, and spectra from
laboratory instrumentation"*. A static printout of the final chromatogram is one rendering of that
data, not the whole of it, and cannot show what an alternative integration would have produced. FDA
makes exactly this point in the 2018 guidance's discussion of static and dynamic records.

**The printout route is therefore not a general escape hatch, and this programme does not treat it as
one.** Where it might legitimately apply at Helix is Phase 07's question, and Phase 07 exists to ask it
honestly rather than to reach for it.

## 8. What Part 11 does not require

The most useful page in this chapter. Every item below is something Part 11 is regularly said to
require and does not. Where Helix chooses to implement any of them, **it is Helix's decision** and this
repository labels it as such.

| Frequently claimed | The actual position |
|---|---|
| **A reason-for-change field on every record edit** | **Not in the text.** `21 CFR 11.10(e)` requires that record changes not obscure previously recorded information. That is a no-overwrite requirement. No section of Part 11 requires a reason to be captured |
| **Logging of read or view events** | **Not in the text.** `21 CFR 11.10(e)` reaches *"operator entries and actions that create, modify, or delete"*. Viewing is not among them |
| **Cryptographic hashing to bind a signature to a record** | **Not in the text.** `21 CFR 11.70` sets the standard as preventing transfer to falsify a record *"by ordinary means"*. Hashing is one way to meet it; the regulation names none |
| **Digital signatures** | **Not required.** `21 CFR 11.3(b)(5)` defines the term; `21 CFR 11.30` names them as an example of an additional measure for open systems. Nowhere are they mandated for closed systems |
| **Multi-factor authentication** | **Not in the text.** `21 CFR 11.200(a)(1)` requires *"at least two distinct identification components"* — an identification code and a password satisfy it |
| **Biometrics** | **Never required.** `21 CFR 11.200(b)` makes biometrics an alternative route, not an obligation |
| **A specific password expiry interval** | **Not in the text.** `21 CFR 11.300(b)` requires that issuances be *"periodically checked, recalled, or revised (e.g., to cover such events as password aging)"* and **specifies no interval**. Any interval is a company procedural decision |
| **Audit trail review at a stated frequency** | **Not a Part 11 requirement at all.** Part 11 requires audit trails to exist, be retained, and be available to the agency. The review obligation and its frequency come from the predicate rules — `01.04 §7` |
| **A "Part 11 certification" for the system or the vendor** | **No such thing exists.** FDA does not certify electronic systems and does not pre-evaluate systems to determine whether they comply with Part 11. There is no FDA registry, list or approval of Part 11 compliant software |

**On that last row, the precise form of words matters.** A vendor may legitimately say that a product
provides **features that support** Part 11 compliance — audit trail generation, signature manifestation
under `21 CFR 11.50`, access control supporting `21 CFR 11.10(d)`. **Compliance is a property of
Helix's validated system in its intended use, under Helix's procedures, operated by Helix's trained
people.** It is not a property a supplier can sell and not something FDA confers. Nothing here is ever
described as "Part 11 certified", "FDA-approved" or "FDA-validated", and outsourcing to a service
provider transfers no responsibility.
